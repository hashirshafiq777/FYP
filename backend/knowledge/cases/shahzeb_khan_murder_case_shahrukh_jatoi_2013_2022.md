# Shahzeb Khan Murder Case (Shahrukh Jatoi v. The State)

**Court:** Anti-Terrorism Court Karachi (trial, 2013); Sindh High Court; Supreme Court of Pakistan (final decision, 2022) **Year:** 2013 to 2022 **Citation:** citation not confirmed

## Background / Facts
On the night of 24 to 25 December 2012, Shahzeb Khan, a 20-year-old student and son of a police officer (DSP Aurangzeb Khan), was shot dead in the Defence Housing Authority area of Karachi after an argument that began when a servant of a neighbouring family allegedly harassed Shahzeb's sister. The accused were Shahrukh Jatoi and Siraj Talpur, sons of influential feudal families, along with Sajjad Talpur and Ghulam Murtaza Lashari. Shahrukh Jatoi fled to Dubai but was brought back after the Chief Justice of Pakistan took suo motu notice following a large social media campaign and protests by civil society in Karachi. On 7 June 2013 an Anti-Terrorism Court in Karachi convicted all four: Shahrukh Jatoi and Siraj Talpur were sentenced to death, and Sajjad Talpur and Ghulam Murtaza Lashari to life imprisonment. In 2013 Shahzeb's parents announced that they had forgiven the accused "in the name of Allah" and entered into a compromise, reportedly after pressure, although the Anti-Terrorism Court held that terrorism offences could not be compounded.

## Legal Issues
- Did the killing amount to "terrorism" under the Anti-Terrorism Act 1997, or was it a private murder arising from a personal dispute?
- Could the offence be compounded (settled) through a compromise and pardon by the victim's legal heirs under the qisas and diyat provisions?
- What was the effect of the compromise on sentences imposed under the Anti-Terrorism Act?

## Decision
In November 2017 the Sindh High Court set aside the Anti-Terrorism Court conviction, holding that the terrorism provisions did not apply, and ordered a retrial before a sessions court; the accused were briefly released on bail, which caused public outrage. In February 2018 the Supreme Court, on petitions by civil society members, set aside the High Court's order and the accused were re-arrested. In 2019 the Sindh High Court, hearing the appeals afresh, commuted the death sentences of Shahrukh Jatoi and Siraj Talpur to life imprisonment. On 18 October 2022 a three-member bench of the Supreme Court held that the offence did not fall within the definition of terrorism under the Anti-Terrorism Act, with the result that the murder offence was compoundable; since the legal heirs had pardoned the accused, the Court accepted the compromise and acquitted all four accused, who were released. The judgment applied the Supreme Court's earlier decision in Ghulam Hussain v. The State (PLD 2020 SC 61), which held that a crime committed for private motives does not become terrorism merely because it causes fear or shock.

## Laws and Provisions Involved
- Section 302(b), Pakistan Penal Code 1860 (intentional murder)
- Section 309, Pakistan Penal Code 1860 (waiver (afw) of qisas by the wali)
- Section 310, Pakistan Penal Code 1860 (compounding of qisas (sulh))
- Section 338-E, Pakistan Penal Code 1860 (waiver or compounding of offences)
- Section 345, Code of Criminal Procedure 1898 (compounding of offences)
- Section 6, Anti-Terrorism Act 1997 (definition of terrorism)
- Section 7, Anti-Terrorism Act 1997 (punishment for acts of terrorism)
- Section 34, Pakistan Penal Code 1860 (common intention)

## Why It Matters
The case became a symbol of the debate over whether Pakistan's qisas and diyat law allows wealthy and powerful accused to escape punishment for murder by pressuring the victim's family into forgiveness. It also clarified, following Ghulam Hussain, that ordinary murders arising out of personal enmity should not be charged under the Anti-Terrorism Act. Advocates for reform cite the case when arguing that the state, not only the heirs, should have a say in murder prosecutions, especially where the pardon may not be voluntary.
