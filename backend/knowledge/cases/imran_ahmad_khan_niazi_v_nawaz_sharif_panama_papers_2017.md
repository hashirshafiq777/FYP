# Imran Ahmad Khan Niazi v. Mian Muhammad Nawaz Sharif (Panama Papers Case)

**Court:** Supreme Court of Pakistan **Year:** 2017 **Citation:** PLD 2017 SC 692 (final judgment of 28 July 2017); PLD 2017 SC 265 (order of 20 April 2017)

## Background / Facts
In April 2016 the Panama Papers leak revealed that the children of Prime Minister Nawaz Sharif (Hussain Nawaz, Hassan Nawaz and Maryam Nawaz) owned offshore companies that held expensive flats in Avenfield House, Park Lane, London. Imran Khan (Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf), Sheikh Rashid Ahmed and Siraj-ul-Haq (Jamaat-e-Islami) filed petitions under Article 184(3) seeking the Prime Minister's disqualification for failing to explain the source of the money and for not being "honest" under Article 62(1)(f). The Sharif family gave differing accounts of how the properties were acquired, including a claim that funds came from the sale of a Gulf Steel Mills business in Dubai and an investment with a Qatari royal family member, supported by letters from that person. A five-member bench heard the case over several months. On 20 April 2017, by a majority of three to two, the Court ordered a Joint Investigation Team (JIT) to investigate the money trail, while the two dissenting judges (Justices Asif Saeed Khosa and Gulzar Ahmed) would have disqualified the Prime Minister immediately. The JIT, which included officers of the FIA, NAB, SECP, State Bank, ISI and MI, filed its report in July 2017.

## Legal Issues
- Could the Supreme Court decide the matter under Article 184(3) rather than leaving it to NAB, the Election Commission or trial courts?
- Had Nawaz Sharif made a false declaration or failed to disclose assets in his nomination papers, so as to be not "honest and ameen" under Article 62(1)(f)?
- Was the explanation of the money trail for the London properties credible?

## Decision
On 28 July 2017 the five-member bench unanimously disqualified Nawaz Sharif from being a member of the National Assembly. The Court found that he had been entitled to receive a salary as chairman of Capital FZE, a company in the UAE owned by his son, and that this receivable salary was an "asset" that he had not declared in his nomination papers for the 2013 election. This non-disclosure made him not honest under Article 62(1)(f) of the Constitution read with the Representation of the People Act 1976, and the Election Commission was directed to de-notify him. The Court also directed the National Accountability Bureau to file references against Nawaz Sharif, his children and son-in-law Captain (retd) Muhammad Safdar, and against Finance Minister Ishaq Dar, within six weeks, and appointed a Supreme Court judge to monitor the trials. Nawaz Sharif resigned the same day. In the Avenfield reference the accountability court convicted him in July 2018 (later set aside by the Islamabad High Court), and in the Al-Azizia reference he was convicted in December 2018 and acquitted on appeal in 2023. In Sami Ullah Baloch v. Abdul Karim Nousherwani (PLD 2018 SC 405) the Court held that disqualification under Article 62(1)(f) is for life, a view it reversed in January 2024.

## Laws and Provisions Involved
- Article 184(3), Constitution of Pakistan 1973
- Article 62(1)(f), Constitution of Pakistan 1973 (qualification of being sagacious, righteous, non-profligate, honest and ameen)
- Section 99(1)(f), Representation of the People Act 1976 (qualifications of candidates)
- Section 12(2)(f), Representation of the People Act 1976 (declaration of assets in nomination papers)
- Section 9(a)(v), National Accountability Ordinance 1999 (assets beyond known sources of income)
- Section 18, National Accountability Ordinance 1999 (filing of references)

## Why It Matters
The Panama case was the first time a sitting Prime Minister of Pakistan was removed by the Supreme Court. It expanded the use of Article 184(3) and the Court's role in investigating alleged corruption through a JIT, which was praised by some as accountability and criticised by others as judicial overreach. The ground of disqualification, an undeclared receivable salary rather than the London flats themselves, remains controversial. It made Article 62(1)(f) a central tool in election disputes and led to further litigation on the length of disqualification.
