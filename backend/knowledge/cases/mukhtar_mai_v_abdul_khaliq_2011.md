# Mukhtar Mai v. Abdul Khaliq (Mukhtaran Mai Gang-Rape Case)

**Court:** Supreme Court of Pakistan **Year:** 2011 **Citation:** PLD 2011 SC 554

## Background / Facts
In June 2002, in the village of Meerwala in Muzaffargarh district, southern Punjab, Mukhtaran Bibi (known as Mukhtar Mai), a woman of the Gujjar community, was gang-raped on the alleged orders of a village council (panchayat) dominated by the more powerful Mastoi clan. The council had been called because her younger brother was accused of an illicit relationship with a Mastoi woman; the rape was described as punishment to restore the Mastoi family's "honour". Unlike many victims, Mukhtar Mai reported the crime, with the support of a local cleric, and the case drew national and international attention. In August 2002 an Anti-Terrorism Court in Dera Ghazi Khan convicted six men (four alleged rapists and two panchayat members) and sentenced them to death, acquitting eight others. In March 2005 the Lahore High Court acquitted five of the six for lack of evidence and reduced the death sentence of Abdul Khaliq, the main accused, to life imprisonment. The Supreme Court suspended the acquittals and the matter remained pending for six years.

## Legal Issues
- Was the evidence of the victim and the prosecution witnesses sufficient to prove gang-rape against each accused beyond reasonable doubt?
- Did the delay in lodging the FIR, and contradictions in the statements, undermine the prosecution case?
- Was the Lahore High Court right to acquit five of the accused and reduce the sentence of the sixth?

## Decision
On 21 April 2011 a three-member bench of the Supreme Court, by a majority of two to one, dismissed Mukhtar Mai's appeal and the state's appeal, upholding the Lahore High Court's acquittal of five accused and the life sentence of Abdul Khaliq. The majority found that the prosecution evidence had material contradictions, that there was a delay of several days in reporting the crime, and that the benefit of doubt had to go to the accused in accordance with settled principles of criminal law. Justice Nasir-ul-Mulk dissented and would have convicted more of the accused. The acquitted men were released after the judgment. The decision was widely criticised by women's rights groups as showing how the criminal justice system fails rape survivors, while the Court maintained that it was bound by the evidence on record. Mukhtar Mai later became an internationally known activist and set up schools and a women's shelter in her village.

## Laws and Provisions Involved
- Offence of Zina (Enforcement of Hudood) Ordinance 1979, Section 10(4) (gang-rape, as it stood at the time of the offence, before the Protection of Women (Criminal Laws Amendment) Act 2006 moved rape to the PPC)
- Section 7, Anti-Terrorism Act 1997 (the trial was held before an Anti-Terrorism Court)
- Section 376, Pakistan Penal Code 1860 (rape, as inserted by the 2006 amendment, relevant to later law)
- Section 149, Pakistan Penal Code 1860 (common object of unlawful assembly) was discussed in the lower courts (exact charge framing not confirmed)

## Why It Matters
The case exposed the role of illegal village councils (panchayats and jirgas) in ordering violence against women and the difficulty of proving rape under the evidentiary rules then in force. It contributed to the Protection of Women (Criminal Laws Amendment) Act 2006, which shifted rape from the Hudood Ordinance to the Pakistan Penal Code, and to later criminal law reforms on rape evidence and medico-legal procedure. The final acquittals are often cited in discussions of the benefit-of-doubt rule and the need for better investigation and witness protection.
