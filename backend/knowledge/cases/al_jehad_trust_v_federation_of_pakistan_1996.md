# Al-Jehad Trust v. Federation of Pakistan (Judges' Case)

**Court:** Supreme Court of Pakistan **Year:** 1996 **Citation:** PLD 1996 SC 324

## Background / Facts
By the mid-1990s the executive had wide discretion in appointing judges to the superior courts. Governments frequently appointed acting Chief Justices for long periods, appointed ad hoc and additional judges, and did not always follow the advice of the Chief Justice of Pakistan. Several petitions were filed under Article 184(3), the leading one by Al-Jehad Trust through its chairman Habib-ul-Wahab-ul-Khairi, challenging appointments and transfers of judges made during the government of Benazir Bhutto, including appointments to the Lahore High Court and the Sindh High Court and the continuation of acting Chief Justices. The petitioners argued that these practices undermined the independence of the judiciary guaranteed by the Constitution. The case was heard by a five-member bench headed by Chief Justice Sajjad Ali Shah.

## Legal Issues
- What is the meaning of "consultation" with the Chief Justice of Pakistan and the Chief Justice of a High Court in Articles 177 and 193 for the appointment of judges?
- Is the senior-most judge of a High Court entitled to be appointed its Chief Justice?
- For how long can acting Chief Justices and ad hoc or additional judges be appointed?
- Do executive appointments made without effective consultation remain valid?

## Decision
The Supreme Court held that the consultation required by the Constitution must be "effective, meaningful, purposive, consensus-oriented" and that the opinion of the Chief Justice of Pakistan on a candidate's fitness is binding on the executive unless very sound reasons are recorded. The Court held that the senior-most judge of a High Court has a legitimate expectancy to be appointed as its Chief Justice, and that appointments of acting Chief Justices should not be prolonged beyond the period needed. It held that the Chief Justice of Pakistan and the Chief Justices of the High Courts should be permanent appointments, that additional judges who perform satisfactorily should normally be confirmed, and that transfers of judges without consent for long periods were not permissible. Several appointments made without proper consultation were declared invalid, though the Court protected past judicial acts. The judgment was announced on 20 March 1996.

## Laws and Provisions Involved
- Article 177, Constitution of Pakistan 1973 (appointment of Supreme Court judges)
- Article 180, Constitution of Pakistan 1973 (acting Chief Justice of Pakistan)
- Article 193, Constitution of Pakistan 1973 (appointment of High Court judges)
- Article 196, Constitution of Pakistan 1973 (acting Chief Justice of a High Court)
- Article 197, Constitution of Pakistan 1973 (additional judges)
- Article 200, Constitution of Pakistan 1973 (transfer of High Court judges)
- Article 184(3), Constitution of Pakistan 1973

## Why It Matters
The Judges' Case is the foundation of modern Pakistani law on judicial independence. It made the judiciary's own voice decisive in judicial appointments, limited the executive's discretion, and established the seniority principle for Chief Justices. These principles were followed in later cases such as the second Al-Jehad Trust case (1997) and Malik Asad Ali v. Federation (1998), and shaped the design of the Judicial Commission introduced by the Eighteenth and Nineteenth Amendments in 2010.
