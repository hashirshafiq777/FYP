# Darshan Masih alias Rehmatay v. The State (Bonded Labour / Brick Kiln Case)

**Court:** Supreme Court of Pakistan **Year:** 1990 **Citation:** PLD 1990 SC 513

## Background / Facts
In 1988 a group of brick kiln workers in Punjab, led by Darshan Masih, sent a telegram to the Chief Justice of Pakistan. They stated that they and their families were being held in bondage by brick kiln owners on account of "peshgi" (advance loans) which they could never repay, that some workers had been abducted and were being held as hostages to force others to return, and that they faced violence from the owners and their guards. They pleaded for freedom and protection. The Supreme Court treated the telegram as a petition under Article 184(3), since it raised questions of public importance about the fundamental rights against slavery and forced labour. The Court issued notices to the Punjab government and police, ordered the recovery of the detained workers, and heard representatives of the workers, the kiln owners and the government over a period of time.

## Legal Issues
- Can the Supreme Court take up a human rights complaint under Article 184(3) on the basis of a telegram or letter, without a formal petition?
- Does the system of advance loans (peshgi) and debt bondage at brick kilns amount to forced labour and slavery prohibited by Article 11 of the Constitution?
- What directions could the Court give to protect the workers and regulate the peshgi system pending legislation?

## Decision
The Supreme Court held that the petition was maintainable and that the bonded labour system practised at the brick kilns violated Article 11, which prohibits slavery and all forms of forced labour, and the right to life and dignity under Articles 9 and 14. The Court ordered that the detained workers be freed, that no worker be compelled to work or be restrained on account of a debt, and that workers were free to leave the kilns. It laid down interim arrangements regarding the peshgi system: the existing advances were to be dealt with as ordinary civil debts recoverable through legal means, future advances were restricted, and the police were directed to act on complaints of coercion. The Court recorded an agreed settlement between the workers and the kiln owners and directed the government to enact legislation. The judgment is usually attributed to the Court led by Chief Justice Muhammad Afzal Zullah, who was associated with the expansion of human rights jurisdiction (authorship not confirmed).

## Laws and Provisions Involved
- Article 11, Constitution of Pakistan 1973 (slavery, forced labour and child labour prohibited)
- Article 9, Constitution of Pakistan 1973 (right to life)
- Article 14, Constitution of Pakistan 1973 (dignity of man)
- Article 184(3), Constitution of Pakistan 1973
- Section 374, Pakistan Penal Code 1860 (unlawful compulsory labour)
- Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act 1992 (enacted as a result of the judgment)

## Why It Matters
Darshan Masih is the leading Pakistani case on bonded labour and one of the earliest examples of "epistolary jurisdiction", where a letter or telegram is treated as a constitutional petition. It directly led to the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act 1992, which abolished the bonded labour system and cancelled bonded debts. The case is also cited as an example of the Supreme Court acting as a human rights court and shaping a remedy through negotiation and supervision rather than only adjudication.
