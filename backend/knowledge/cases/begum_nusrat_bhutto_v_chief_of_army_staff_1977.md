# Begum Nusrat Bhutto v. Chief of Army Staff

**Court:** Supreme Court of Pakistan **Year:** 1977 **Citation:** PLD 1977 SC 657

## Background / Facts
After disputed general elections in March 1977 and months of protests by the Pakistan National Alliance, General Muhammad Zia-ul-Haq, the Chief of Army Staff, removed Prime Minister Zulfikar Ali Bhutto on 5 July 1977 (Operation Fair Play), held the 1973 Constitution "in abeyance" and imposed martial law. In September 1977 Bhutto and several leaders of the Pakistan Peoples Party were detained under Martial Law Order No. 12. Begum Nusrat Bhutto, Bhutto's wife, filed a petition directly in the Supreme Court under Article 184(3) challenging the detentions and the legality of the military takeover itself. The Chief Justice Yaqub Ali, who was expected to hear the case, was removed by a martial law order and Justice Anwarul Haq became Chief Justice.

## Legal Issues
- Was the military takeover of 5 July 1977 and the imposition of martial law valid?
- Were the detentions of Bhutto and other leaders under Martial Law Order No. 12 lawful?
- Did the superior courts retain jurisdiction to review martial law actions?

## Decision
The Supreme Court, by a nine-member bench headed by Chief Justice Anwarul Haq, dismissed the petition. The Court held that the takeover was justified by the doctrine of state necessity, because a constitutional and political breakdown had left no other way to restore order and hold elections. It distinguished Asma Jilani by saying that the 1977 situation was an extra-constitutional step taken for the welfare of the people and for a temporary period, not a permanent usurpation. The Court accepted the Chief Martial Law Administrator's statement that he intended to hold elections and hand power back soon. It also held that the courts kept their power of judicial review and that the regime could act only within the limits of necessity. Bhutto's detention was not interfered with, and in practice elections were postponed for many years.

## Laws and Provisions Involved
- Article 184(3), Constitution of Pakistan 1973 (original jurisdiction of the Supreme Court in matters of public importance involving fundamental rights)
- Laws (Continuance in Force) Order 1977
- Martial Law Order No. 12 of 1977 (detention)
- Article 6, Constitution of Pakistan 1973 (high treason) was discussed in context

## Why It Matters
The case revived the doctrine of necessity in a broad form only five years after Asma Jilani had restricted it, and provided legal cover for Zia-ul-Haq's eleven-year rule. It is often criticised as the point at which the judiciary gave a military ruler almost unlimited power, including the power to amend the Constitution, which was later used in the Eighth Amendment. Along with Dosso and Zafar Ali Shah, it forms the trio of cases that validated military rule in Pakistan.
