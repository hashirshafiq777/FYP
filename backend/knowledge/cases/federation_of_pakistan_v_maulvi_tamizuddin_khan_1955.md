# Federation of Pakistan v. Maulvi Tamizuddin Khan

**Court:** Federal Court of Pakistan **Year:** 1955 **Citation:** PLD 1955 FC 240

## Background / Facts
On 24 October 1954, Governor-General Ghulam Muhammad dissolved the first Constituent Assembly of Pakistan, which had been working since 1947 on a constitution and had recently passed amendments limiting the Governor-General's powers. Maulvi Tamizuddin Khan, the President (Speaker) of the Constituent Assembly, challenged the dissolution before the Chief Court of Sindh, seeking writs of mandamus and quo warranto under Section 223-A of the Government of India Act 1935, a section the Assembly had itself inserted. The Sindh Chief Court ruled in his favour and declared the dissolution unlawful. The Federation appealed to the Federal Court, headed by Chief Justice Muhammad Munir.

## Legal Issues
- Could the Governor-General lawfully dissolve the Constituent Assembly?
- Did the Chief Court of Sindh have jurisdiction to issue the writs under Section 223-A of the Government of India Act 1935?
- Did laws passed by the Constituent Assembly in its constitution-making capacity require the assent of the Governor-General to become valid?

## Decision
The Federal Court, by majority, allowed the Federation's appeal on a technical ground. It held that Section 223-A, which gave the Sindh Chief Court the power to issue writs, had never received the assent of the Governor-General and therefore was not valid law, so the Chief Court had no jurisdiction to grant relief. The Court reasoned that constitutional legislation passed by the Constituent Assembly also required the Governor-General's assent under the Indian Independence Act 1947. The result was that the dissolution of the Assembly stood. Justice A. R. Cornelius wrote a strong dissent, holding that the Assembly's constitutional acts did not need assent. The decision cast doubt on dozens of laws passed since 1947, which led to the Usif Patel case and then the Governor-General's Reference (Reference No. 1 of 1955), where the Federal Court first applied the doctrine of necessity.

## Laws and Provisions Involved
- Section 223-A, Government of India Act 1935 (as adapted for Pakistan), writ jurisdiction of High Courts
- Sections 6 and 8, Indian Independence Act 1947, powers of the Constituent Assembly and the Governor-General
- Section 5, Indian Independence Act 1947, office of Governor-General

## Why It Matters
This was the first major constitutional crisis of Pakistan and the first time the superior judiciary upheld an executive act that removed an elected constitution-making body. It opened the path for the doctrine of necessity, which was formally used in the 1955 Reference and later in Dosso (1958). Legal historians treat it as the beginning of a pattern in which courts validated extra-constitutional actions. The dissent of Justice Cornelius is often cited as the correct constitutional view.

## Simple Explanation (Roman Urdu)
1954 mein Governor-General Ghulam Muhammad ne Pakistan ki pehli Constituent Assembly tor di. Assembly ke Speaker Maulvi Tamizuddin Khan Sindh Chief Court gaye aur jeet gaye, lekin Federal Court ne yeh faisla ulat diya. Federal Court ne kaha ke jis qanoon ke tahat Sindh court ne hukm diya tha, us par Governor-General ne dastakhat nahi kiye thay, is liye woh qanoon hi valid nahi tha. Is tarah Assembly ki barkhastagi barqarar rahi aur yahi se "nazriya-e-zaroorat" (doctrine of necessity) ki bunyad pari.
