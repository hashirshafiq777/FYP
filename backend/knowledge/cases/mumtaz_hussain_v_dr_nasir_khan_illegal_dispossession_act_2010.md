# Mumtaz Hussain v. Dr. Nasir Khan (Scope of the Illegal Dispossession Act 2005)

**Court:** Supreme Court of Pakistan **Year:** 2010 **Citation:** 2010 SCMR 1254 (not confirmed)

## Background / Facts
The Illegal Dispossession Act 2005 was enacted to protect lawful owners and occupiers of immovable property from being forcibly or illegally dispossessed by "property grabbers" and the "qabza mafia". Section 3 makes it an offence for any person to enter upon or into property with intent to dispossess the owner or occupier without lawful authority, and Section 4 makes the offence punishable with imprisonment of up to ten years and fine. The Act gives the Court of Session jurisdiction to try complaints directly, bypassing the ordinary police investigation route, and permits restoration of possession. After the Act came into force, several High Courts held that it applied only to professional land grabbers and organised groups (qabza mafia), relying on the preamble, and not to ordinary disputes between relatives, tenants and landlords or neighbours; other courts disagreed. In this case, Dr. Nasir Khan filed a complaint under the Act against Mumtaz Hussain alleging illegal dispossession from property; the accused argued that he was not a land grabber in the professional sense and that the Act therefore did not apply to him.

## Legal Issues
- Does the Illegal Dispossession Act 2005 apply to every person who illegally dispossesses an owner or occupier, or only to professional land grabbers and organised qabza groups?
- Can the preamble of a statute restrict the plain meaning of its operative section?
- Could a complaint under the Act proceed where there is an existing dispute about title or tenancy between the parties?

## Decision
The Supreme Court held that the Act is not limited to the qabza mafia or professional land grabbers and that Section 3 applies to any person who, without lawful authority, enters property with the intention of dispossessing the lawful owner or occupier. The Court reasoned that the language of Section 3 is clear and general, that a preamble cannot cut down the plain words of an enacting provision, and that the mischief the law seeks to remedy, forcible dispossession, is the same whoever commits it. The Court therefore rejected the argument that the complaint was not maintainable against an individual who was not a habitual land grabber. At the same time the Court noted that the complainant must show that he was in lawful possession or ownership and that the dispossession was without lawful authority, and that a genuine civil dispute about title should be decided by the civil court. The appeal of Mumtaz Hussain was dismissed and the proceedings under the Act were allowed to continue.

## Laws and Provisions Involved
- Illegal Dispossession Act 2005, Section 3 (prevention of illegal possession of property)
- Illegal Dispossession Act 2005, Section 4 (punishment for illegal dispossession, up to ten years and fine)
- Illegal Dispossession Act 2005, Section 5 (court of Session to have jurisdiction; procedure on complaint)
- Illegal Dispossession Act 2005, Section 7 (investigation and prosecution)
- Illegal Dispossession Act 2005, Section 8 (restoration of possession to the owner or occupier)
- Section 441, Pakistan Penal Code 1860 (criminal trespass), compared

## Why It Matters
This decision settled the conflict among High Courts about the scope of the Illegal Dispossession Act and confirmed that it is a general remedy against forcible dispossession, available against relatives, co-sharers, former tenants and others, not only against organised land mafias. It made the Act one of the most used property-related criminal remedies in Pakistan. Later Supreme Court decisions, including those on the date of dispossession and on the need for lawful possession by the complainant, build on this case.
