# Asma Jilani v. Government of the Punjab

**Court:** Supreme Court of Pakistan **Year:** 1972 **Citation:** PLD 1972 SC 139

## Background / Facts
In 1969 General Yahya Khan took over power from Ayub Khan, abrogated the 1962 Constitution and imposed martial law. Under his regime, Malik Ghulam Jilani (a political leader and father of Asma Jilani) and Altaf Gauhar (a senior civil servant and journalist) were detained under Martial Law Regulation No. 78. Asma Jilani filed a habeas corpus petition in the Lahore High Court for her father's release, and Zarina Gauhar filed a similar petition in the Sindh High Court for her husband. Both High Courts dismissed the petitions, relying on the Dosso judgment and on the Jurisdiction of Courts (Removal of Doubts) Order 1969, which barred courts from questioning martial law actions. The petitioners appealed to the Supreme Court after Yahya Khan had already handed power to Zulfikar Ali Bhutto in December 1971.

## Legal Issues
- Was Yahya Khan's assumption of power in 1969 and his martial law legally valid?
- Was the Dosso principle, based on Kelsen's theory of revolutionary legality, correct?
- Could the courts be barred from reviewing detentions ordered under martial law regulations?

## Decision
The Supreme Court, led by Chief Justice Hamoodur Rahman, unanimously allowed the appeals and overruled Dosso. It held that Kelsen's theory was not a universally accepted rule of law and could not be used to legitimise a usurper. Yahya Khan was declared a usurper who had seized power unconstitutionally, and his martial law and the Jurisdiction of Courts (Removal of Doubts) Order 1969 were held to be without lawful authority. The detentions were declared illegal. The Court said that acts of the usurper could be "condoned" only to the extent they were necessary to keep the state running and were for the welfare of the people, which is the narrow version of the doctrine of necessity. The Court also stated that the legal sovereignty in Pakistan belongs to Allah and is exercised through the people, as reflected in the Objectives Resolution.

## Laws and Provisions Involved
- Martial Law Regulation No. 78 (1971), preventive detention
- Jurisdiction of Courts (Removal of Doubts) Order 1969
- Provisional Constitution Order 1969
- Article 98, Constitution of Pakistan 1962 (writ jurisdiction of High Courts)
- Objectives Resolution 1949

## Why It Matters
Asma Jilani is the most celebrated statement by the Pakistani judiciary against military usurpation of power. It overruled Dosso and established that courts should not give legitimacy to unconstitutional seizures of power, and that the doctrine of necessity may only condone acts that are strictly necessary. Later courts, however, returned to a wider doctrine of necessity in Nusrat Bhutto (1977) and Zafar Ali Shah (2000). The case remains the leading authority for the principle that the Constitution, and not force, is the source of lawful authority.
