# Federation of Pakistan v. Mst. Farishta (Section 4 MFLO, Orphaned Grandchildren)

**Court:** Peshawar High Court, Shariat Bench (1980); Supreme Court of Pakistan, Shariat Appellate Bench (1981) **Year:** 1980 to 1981 **Citation:** PLD 1980 Peshawar 47 (Peshawar High Court); PLD 1981 SC 120 (Supreme Court)

## Background / Facts
Section 4 of the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961 provides that if a son or daughter of the propositus (the person whose estate is being distributed) dies before the opening of succession, the children of that deceased child receive the share that their parent would have received had he or she been alive. This departs from the classical Sunni law of inheritance, under which such orphaned grandchildren are excluded by the surviving sons of the deceased. In 1979 and 1980, Shariat Benches were created in the High Courts to examine whether laws were repugnant to the injunctions of Islam. Mst. Farishta filed a petition in the Shariat Bench of the Peshawar High Court challenging Section 4 as repugnant to the Islamic law of inheritance. The Peshawar High Court accepted the petition and declared Section 4 repugnant to Islam. The Federation appealed to the Shariat Appellate Bench of the Supreme Court.

## Legal Issues
- Did the Shariat Bench have jurisdiction to examine the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance, given that the constitutional definition of "law" for Shariat review excluded "Muslim personal law"?
- Is the MFLO, a statute enacted by the state, part of "Muslim personal law" within the meaning of that exclusion?
- Is Section 4 of the MFLO repugnant to the injunctions of Islam?

## Decision
The Peshawar High Court Shariat Bench held that Section 4 was contrary to the Quranic scheme of inheritance and declared it repugnant. On appeal, the Shariat Appellate Bench of the Supreme Court reversed that decision on the question of jurisdiction. The Supreme Court held that the expression "Muslim personal law" in the constitutional provision (then Article 203B, as inserted by the President's orders of 1979 and 1980) referred to the personal law of Muslims as applied to them by statutes, which included the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance, and that such laws were therefore excluded from the jurisdiction of the Shariat courts. The Court did not decide whether Section 4 was actually repugnant to Islam, and the section remained in force. The jurisdiction question was later changed by constitutional amendment, which made it possible for the Federal Shariat Court to examine the MFLO in Allah Rakha v. Federation (2000).

## Laws and Provisions Involved
- Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961, Section 4 (succession by children of a predeceased son or daughter)
- Article 203B, Constitution of Pakistan 1973 (definition of "law" for the purpose of Shariat jurisdiction, as it stood in 1980 and 1981, excluding Muslim personal law)
- Article 203D, Constitution of Pakistan 1973 (power of the Federal Shariat Court to examine laws)
- Constitution (Amendment) Order 1979 and Constitution (Amendment) Order 1980 (creation of Shariat Benches and the Federal Shariat Court)
- Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act 1962 (West Pakistan) (application of Muslim personal law to inheritance)

## Why It Matters
Farishta is the leading case on what counts as "Muslim personal law" and on the limits of the Federal Shariat Court's jurisdiction over family law statutes. For almost twenty years it shielded the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance from Shariat review, which preserved reforms such as Section 4 for orphaned grandchildren and the talaq notice rules. It remains important for understanding the later Allah Rakha litigation and for the interpretation of Article 203B.

## Simple Explanation (Roman Urdu)
Muslim Family Laws Ordinance ka Section 4 kehta hai ke agar kisi ka beta ya beti us se pehle faut ho jaye to us ke bachay (yateem potay-potiyan ya nawasay-nawasiyan) dada ki wirasat mein apne walid ya walida ka hissa payenge. Mst. Farishta ne Peshawar High Court ki Shariat Bench mein kaha ke yeh Islam ke khilaf hai aur High Court ne yeh maan liya. Lekin Supreme Court ne 1981 mein kaha ke yeh Ordinance "Muslim personal law" hai jise Shariat court jaanch hi nahi sakti, is liye High Court ka faisla khatam kar diya aur Section 4 lagu raha.
