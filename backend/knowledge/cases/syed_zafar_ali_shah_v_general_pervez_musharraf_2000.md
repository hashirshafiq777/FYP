# Syed Zafar Ali Shah v. General Pervez Musharraf, Chief Executive of Pakistan

**Court:** Supreme Court of Pakistan **Year:** 2000 **Citation:** PLD 2000 SC 869

## Background / Facts
On 12 October 1999, after Prime Minister Nawaz Sharif attempted to remove General Pervez Musharraf as Army Chief while he was abroad, the army took control of the country. Musharraf declared a state of emergency, held the Constitution in abeyance through the Provisional Constitution Order No. 1 of 1999, dissolved the National and Provincial Assemblies and assumed the title of Chief Executive. In January 2000 judges of the superior courts were required to take a fresh oath under the Oath of Office (Judges) Order 2000; several judges, including Chief Justice Saeeduzzaman Siddiqui, refused and ceased to hold office. Syed Zafar Ali Shah, a PML-N member of the dissolved National Assembly, and others challenged the takeover in the Supreme Court. The case was heard by a twelve-member bench headed by Chief Justice Irshad Hasan Khan, all of whom had taken the new oath.

## Legal Issues
- Was the military takeover of 12 October 1999 and the Provisional Constitution Order valid?
- Could the Chief Executive amend the Constitution, and for how long could he rule?
- Did the superior courts retain jurisdiction to review the acts of the regime?

## Decision
On 12 May 2000 the Supreme Court validated the takeover on the basis of the doctrine of state necessity, holding that the situation on 12 October 1999 had left no constitutional way out, and that the regime was to be allowed to govern for a limited period to achieve its declared objectives. The Court gave the Chief Executive three years from 12 October 1999 to hold general elections, with ninety days after that for the transfer of power. It held that the Chief Executive could amend the Constitution only to the extent necessary for his objectives, but could not alter its salient features, namely the independence of the judiciary, federalism and the parliamentary form of government blended with Islamic provisions. The Court stated that it kept its power of judicial review over the regime's actions. The removal of judges who refused the new oath was not disturbed.

## Laws and Provisions Involved
- Proclamation of Emergency of 14 October 1999
- Provisional Constitution Order No. 1 of 1999
- Oath of Office (Judges) Order 2000
- Article 184(3), Constitution of Pakistan 1973
- Article 6, Constitution of Pakistan 1973 (high treason), discussed but not applied
- Articles 58 and 112, Constitution of Pakistan 1973 (dissolution of assemblies)

## Why It Matters
This case is the third major judgment in which the Supreme Court used the doctrine of necessity to legitimise a military takeover, after Dosso (1958) and Nusrat Bhutto (1977). It is notable for setting a time limit and for the Court's attempt to define limits on the power to amend the Constitution. Those limits were tested when Musharraf later held a referendum, issued the Legal Framework Order 2002 and obtained the Seventeenth Amendment, and imposed a second emergency in 2007. In the Sindh High Court Bar Association case (2009) the Court moved away from the necessity doctrine, and the Eighteenth Amendment added to Article 6 that no court shall validate high treason.

12 October 1999 ko General Musharraf ne Nawaz Sharif ki hukumat khatam kar ke iqtidar sambhal liya. Zafar Ali Shah aur doosron ne Supreme Court mein is ko challenge kiya. Supreme Court ne 2000 mein kaha ke halaat ki "zaroorat" ki wajah se yeh qadam jaiz hai, lekin Musharraf ko teen saal ke andar elections karwane honge, aur woh aain ki bunyadi khususiyat, jaise adliya ki azadi aur parliamentary nizam, tabdeel nahi kar sakte.
