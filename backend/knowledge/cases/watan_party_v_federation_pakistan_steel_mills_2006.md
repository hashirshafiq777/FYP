# Watan Party v. Federation of Pakistan (Pakistan Steel Mills Privatisation Case)

**Court:** Supreme Court of Pakistan **Year:** 2006 (judgment of 23 June 2006) **Citation:** PLD 2006 SC 697

## Background / Facts
Pakistan Steel Mills Corporation, in Karachi, is the country's largest industrial complex and was built with Soviet assistance in the 1970s and 1980s. In 2005 and 2006 the government of General Pervez Musharraf and Prime Minister Shaukat Aziz decided to privatise it through the Privatisation Commission. On 31 March 2006 the Commission approved the sale of 75 percent of the shares to a consortium of Arif Habib Securities, Al-Tuwairqi Group of Saudi Arabia and Magnitogorsk Iron and Steel Works of Russia for about Rs 21.68 billion. Critics argued that the price was far below the value of the mills, which had thousands of acres of land, a port facility and large stocks, and that the valuation had been changed shortly before the bidding, the reference price lowered, and the process rushed. Watan Party and others, and the Steel Mills workers' union, challenged the sale under Article 184(3). A nine-member bench headed by Chief Justice Iftikhar Muhammad Chaudhry heard the case.

## Legal Issues
- Did the Supreme Court have jurisdiction under Article 184(3) to examine a commercial decision of the executive to privatise a public enterprise?
- Was the privatisation process carried out in accordance with the Privatisation Commission Ordinance 2000 and its rules, including proper valuation and approval by the Cabinet Committee on Privatisation?
- Did the Commission's decisions suffer from irregularities, haste and failure to consider relevant material such as the value of the land and the current assets?

## Decision
The Supreme Court unanimously set aside the sale. It held that while privatisation policy is for the government to decide, the process must comply with the law and the Court can review whether the statutory procedure was followed and whether the decision was arbitrary. The Court found that the Privatisation Commission had not properly valued the assets, that the reference price was set without taking into account the land and other assets and the mills' improved financial position, that the Cabinet Committee on Privatisation approved the transaction in indecent haste, and that the consortium had been permitted to change its composition after pre-qualification. These were held to be violations of the Privatisation Commission Ordinance 2000 and of the duty of transparency in dealing with public property. The letter of acceptance and the sale were declared void, and the government was directed to carry out any future privatisation strictly according to law. The Court described its role as ensuring that public assets are not disposed of in a manner that is unfair and non-transparent.

## Laws and Provisions Involved
- Article 184(3), Constitution of Pakistan 1973
- Privatisation Commission Ordinance 2000, in particular Sections 5, 6, 22 and 23 (functions of the Commission, modes of privatisation and approval by the Cabinet)
- Privatisation Commission (Modes and Procedure) Rules 2001
- Article 173, Constitution of Pakistan 1973 (power of the government to dispose of property)
- Article 4, Constitution of Pakistan 1973 (right to be treated in accordance with law)

## Why It Matters
The Steel Mills case is the leading authority on judicial review of privatisation and of executive economic decisions in Pakistan. It established that public assets must be sold through a transparent and lawful process and that the Supreme Court will intervene where statutory procedures are ignored. The judgment is widely seen as the beginning of open conflict between Chief Justice Iftikhar Chaudhry and the Musharraf government, which led to the Chief Justice's suspension in March 2007 and the lawyers' movement. Pakistan Steel Mills was never privatised and later became a heavy burden on the public exchequer, which critics of the judgment point to in arguing that courts should be cautious in economic matters.
